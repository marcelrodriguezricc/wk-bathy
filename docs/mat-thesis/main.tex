\section{Introduction}

% ---------- 1.1 General Background ----------
\subsection{Background}
Since the earliest record of coastal human settlement on the African coast over 100,000 years ago, the coastal zone has been a cradle for human development, to the extent that adaptations to coastal environments are theorized to have influenced human evolutionary traits.~\cite{Will2016} Coasts provide for civilization in several ways---as an abundant source of food and energy resources and as a gateway for travel, commerce, exploration, and recreation---and our understanding of coastal processes has been intrinsically linked to our development as a civilization and species. That token extends into the modern era, as today, the coastal zone is inhabited by approximately 40\% of the global population, and is host to much of our most critical infrastructure.~\cite{Reithmeier2025}

Most mega-cities are located in the Low Elevation Coastal Zone (LECZ), that represents 2\% of the worlds total land area inhabited by 11\% of the global population.~\cite{Neumann2015} The number of people living in the LECZ has increased by 200 million from 1990 to 2015, and is projected to reach 1 billion people by 2050.~\cite{Neumann2015} The majority of natural disasters occur either as a direct cause of marine processes, by meteorological events that occur over the ocean, or by geological events that happen at plate boundaries located at continental edges and mid-ocean ridges.~\cite{Kron2013} Cities in the LECZ are especially vulnerable to the damaging effects of coastal processes exacerbated by warming global climates---namely sea level rise, coastal erosion, and flooding---and so there is a growing need for informed solutions that can mitigate their impact.~\cite{Neumann2015, Day2023} Physical models of coastal processes that enable accurate forecasting and analysis are at the crux of coastal science; they are used to inform and support a wide variety of applications, including---weather and flood prediction, infrastructure planning and management, aquaculture and fishing, maritime safety and efficiency, naval operation, and biogeophysical parameter estimation.~\cite{DeMeyFreemaux2009, Vitousek2023, VanDongeren2008}

% ---------- 1.2 Challenges with measuring coastal phenomena ----------
\subsection{Challenges with measuring coastal phenomena}
Coastal environments are exceptionally dynamic---there are several transformative marine and land phenomena continuously occurring on a range of spatial-temporal scales, simultaneously influencing hydro- and morphological dynamics to various extents across discrete coastal areas.~\cite{Hunt2023} Coastal science is often referred to as being data-poor, as turbulent marine processes and unstable morphology make sampling environmental parameters difficult.~\cite{Vitousek2023, Holman2013} Traditionally, the network of coastal monitoring systems had been limited to only a few specific sites that are highly-instrumented with in-situ point sensors. While highly accurate, these in-situ point sensors are limited in the several ways:

 {\small
 \begin{itemize}
    \item High cost associated with equipment, maintenance, and deployment
    \item Highly localized, small area of coverage
    \item Nearshore phenomena are highly inhomogeneous in the along-shore direction, requiring a large sensor array to achieve adequate spatial resolution
    \item Rapid erosion and accretion in the nearshore can dislodge or bury moored instruments
    \item Large tidal sea-level changes can move in‐situ sensors out of range for meaningful data collection
\end{itemize}
}
~\cite{Vitousek2023, Holman2013}

Coastal environments are highly inhomogeneous in the along-shore direction, and so the effective range of measurements taken from one area for modeling phenomena in another are highly limited based on a variety of environmental parameters.~\cite{Holman2013}. The limitations of the current network of coastal sensing systems prohibits accurate predictive modeling of coastal phenomena on a global scale, and so solutions are needed to expand the amount of available measurement data on coastal phenomena.

% ---------- 1.3 Satellite-based remote sensing as a solution ----------
\subsection{Satellite-based remote sensing as a solution}
To address the lack of coverage granted by the network of coastal monitoring systems, many coastal researchers have directed their efforts toward using data products born of the current array of Earth-observing satellites to measure coastal phenomena.~\cite{Vitousek2023, Holman2013} Satellite-based remote sensing offers a promising alternative to traditional methods for monitoring in-situ point sensors for overcoming the challenges with measuring coastal phenomena:

{\small
\begin{itemize}
    \item Data products are available to public for free
    \item Provides global coverage
    \item Able to simultaneously sample a wide spatial array of points
    \item Sensors are removed from damaging and destabilizing hydrodynamic and morphological processes
\end{itemize}
}
~\cite{Vitousek2023, Holman2013}

Internationally, governmental space agencies conduct satellite missions that generate rigorously calibrated and well-documented data products. These products are made openly available as a public good in support of independent scientific research and applied uses. The purposes of these missions vary, and accordingly each satellite operates under a distinct set of mission parameters, including---the suite of Earth-observing sensors and supporting instruments onboard, the type of orbit about Earth, and the altitude from which observations are made. Together, these factors yield resulting data products that are vastly different from one another. Historically, satellite imagery had limited utility for coastal science due to inadequate spatial and temporal resolution, and the lack of sensitivity of early sensors to the weak reflectance signals from aquatic surfaces that transmit and absorb much of incident radiation into the water column. However, modern developments in sensor technology have increased sensitivity across electromagnetic spectral bands for which water exhibits strong reflective properties; advances in data assimilation techniques combine disparate data sources to improve the quality and accuracy of derived products; innovations in satellite technology have led to an unprecedented number of satellites deployed in Earth orbit; and advances in computational methods now allow physical properties of surface phenomena to be inferred from measurements of incident electromagnetic radiation. Collectively, these advancements have made the use of Earth-observing satellite data products a promising prospect for expanding the amount of available data for numerical modeling of coastal processes.



% ---------- 1.4 Bathymetry ----------
\subsection{Bathymetry}
Bathymetry is the variable that currently limits numerical modeling of nearshore processes.~\cite{Holman2013, VanDongeren2008} Currently, less than ~15\% of the ocean floor, representing ~70\% of the area of Earth’s crust, has been mapped at a spatial resolution of under 5 kilometers.~\cite{Baumann2019} Hydro-dynamic processes in the nearshore such as currents and wave dissipation are highly subject to even small changes in bathymetric morphology, and so models predicting nearshore dynamics improve markedly when detailed bathymetric data is provided.~\cite{Ruessink2001}~\cite{VanDongeren2008} Under certain conditions, bathymetry can be highly variable, so up-to-date information is also crucial to accurate modeling.~\cite{VanDongeren2008}

While both active and passive satellite remote sensors are unable to penetrate the surface of the ocean in all but the shallowest and least turbid areas, characteristics of the seafloor can be derived from images of surface phenomena by retrieval algorithms.~\cite{Holman2013, Reithmeier2025} \textit{Wave-kinematics bathymetric inversion} is a method by which spatial variance in wave celerity across an image and the relationship between wave celerity and depth given by linear or non-linear dispersion equations are used to derive depth. The ability of wave-kinematics bathymetric inversion algorithms to derive physical bathymetric measurements from data gathered remotely by sensors operating outside of the reach of volatile nearshore processes has made it the focus of recent efforts, for its promise toward extending the extents of bathymetric maps and the frequency at which they are updated.~\cite{ODea2025}. 

% ---------- 1.5 The role of Media Arts ----------
\subsection{The role of Media Arts}
Wave-kinematic bathymetric inversion relies on image and spatial information processing techniques to extract the dominant wave frequency at several points across an image, the effectiveness of which is highly dependent on environmental and observational conditions.~\cite{VanDongeren2008} The requirement that these factors align favorably significantly limits the availability of suitable satellite imagery for wave-kinematic inversion. However, advances in image and spatial information processing methods can extend the utility of imagery acquired under sub-optimal conditions, increasing the overall applicability of the technique. To that end, insights from the field of Media Arts—--for which multimedia systems engineering, computational modeling, and image processing are core subjects---are particularly valuable for expanding the range of imagery suitable for accurate wave-kinematic bathymetric inversion, thereby increasing the spatial extent of up-to-date bathymetric data.

% ---------- 1.6 Objectives ----------
\subsection{Objectives}

The specificity of wave-kinematic bathymetric inversion requires that improvements to the algorithms implementing this technique be informed by an understanding of both the governing physical processes and the remote sensing systems used to acquire the imagery. The objectives of this paper are to synthesize existing knowledge of the hydro- and morphodynamic phenomena relevant to wave-kinematic bathymetric inversion; to review applicable satellite platforms and sensing techniques from which suitable imagery may be obtained; to present a modular Python toolbox developed for experimentation with wave kinematic inversion pipelines across different areas of interest and processing strategies; and to evaluate the effectiveness of these techniques under varying environmental conditions, in support of efforts to expand the availability and spatial coverage of detailed, up-to-date bathymetric data. Additionally, this paper is intended to serve as a framework through which practitioners in the field of Media Arts may apply their skills to interdisciplinary scientific problem solving.

\section{Pertinent coastal phenomena}

% ---------- 2.1 Defining the coast ----------
\subsection{Defining the coast}
Broadly, a coast is the zone extending both landward and seaward from the shoreline, defined as the boundary where the ocean meets the land. Discrepancies exist among organizations regarding how the coastal zone is defined and how its spatial extent is understood. For example, some sources define the coastal zone according to a fixed distance from the shoreline~\cite{NOAACoastalWatersGlossary}, while others define coasts by the spatial reach of processes that arise from the interaction between land and sea~\cite{LibreTexts}. Within these general differences lie more specific ones. In the former case, regulatory bodies in different countries may delineate the shoreline using different tidal reference lines~\cite{PellachAlterman2021}; in the latter, studies from different disciplines may include or exclude particular processes depending on the timescale of interest~\cite{LibreTexts}.

The nebulousness of definitions for the coast are a cause of the numerous factors that contribute to its morphology, and the sensitivity of which factors are considered to the success of an applied use-case. Hydro-dynamic processes occurring at different timescales work simultaneously to continuously transform coasts: from short-term transport of sediment caused by turbulance in the nearshore, to long term eustatic sea level changes which redefine global shorelines and ocean basins from processes such as glaciation, volcanic outgassing, and plate tectonics.~\ref{figure1} Carefully defining the spatiotemporal extent of relevant coastal processes with respect to the intended application is integral to effective modeling of coastal processes. For example, models that explicitly incorporate equilibrium concepts have proven more successful in predicting short- to medium-term coastal behavior than processes operating on longer, multi-decadal timescales~\cite{Hunt2023}.

% ---------- 2.2 Wind generated ocean waves ----------
\subsection{Wind generated ocean waves}

Wind-generated surface waves originate from aerodynamic pressure fluctuations and shear stresses exerted by wind at the air–sea interface. At very small scales, surface tension acts as the dominant restoring force, giving rise to capillary waves characterized by low energy and high frequency. These capillary waves increase surface roughness, enhancing momentum transfer from the atmosphere to the ocean and facilitating the growth of longer waves. Once wavelengths exceed approximately 1.7 cm, gravity becomes the dominant restoring force, and the waves transition to gravity waves. In this regime, displaced water parcels oscillate about their equilibrium positions in approximately orbital motions as gravitational forces act to restore the surface wave and momentum causes the wave to overshoot.~\cite{Garrison2012, Masselink2014, Kinsman1984}

Over time, high-frequency wave components dissipate and nonlinear interactions redistribute energy toward lower frequencies. The surface of deep water is said to be a free surface, as the orbital path of water molecules are governed only by gravity, intertia, and pressure forces, free of mechanical constraints imposed by a solid boundary. Water with a free surface is a dispersive medium, meaning waves of different wavelengths propagating on the water's surface travel at different phase speeds. By the process of dispersion, waves are sorted into packets of uniform wavelength as they travel away from the area of generation, producing a swell. The virtually frictionless orbital motion of swell waves allows them to propagate over long distances across the ocean, until they meet an obstacle or enter the nearshore zone.~\cite{Garrison2012, Masselink2014, Kinsman1984, PiersonMoskowitz1964}

As swell waves enter the nearshore, decreasing water depth relative to wavelength induces shoaling, increasing wave height and slowing orbital velocities until depth-limited breaking occurs.~\cite{HartMiskin1945} The relationship between depth and wave speed can be expressed by the theoretical linear dispersion equation for surface gravity waves:

\begin{equation}
\label{dispersion}
\sigma^2 = gk \tanh(kh)
\end{equation}
~\cite{Holland2001, HartMiskin1945}

where:

{\small
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\sigma$ is radial frequency is defined as $2π/T$, where T is the wave period
    \item $k$ is the wavenumber ($k = 2π∕L$, where $L$ is the wavelength)
    \item $h$ is the water depth
    \item and $g$ is the gravitational constant $\approx 9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$
\end{itemize}
}

~\cite{Holland2001, HartMiskin1945}

Wave-kinematic bathymetric inversion algorithms that employ satellite imagery typically partition the image into \textit{N × N} pixel subsets and apply a two-dimensional Fourier transform to each subset. From these spectra, the wavelength 
$L$ of the dominant sea waves is estimated at the centroid of each subset across the full image extent. The wave period $T$ is obtained from an external source, such as a nearby wave buoy or a historical wave model. An algebraic rearrangement of the linear dispersion relation is then used to solve for the local water depth $h$ at each subset centroid:

\begin{equation}
h(\lambda, T_p) = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi} \,
\operatorname{atanh}\!\left( \frac{2\pi \lambda}{g T_p^2} \right)
\label{depth_inversion}
\end{equation}
~\cite{Mudiyanselage2024, Bergsma2019} 

Most wave energy is dissipated within a relatively narrow coastal band, often on the order of 100 meters in width, where energy dissipation rates of approximately 10 – 100 watts per square meter, making the surf zone one of the most energetic regions of the ocean.~\cite{Holman2013} The surf zone defines the practical limit of wave-kinematic bathymetric inversion in the across-shore direction, as wave breaking and rapid energy loss degrade the assumptions required for dispersion-based depth estimation. The exact across-shore extent of this limitation is site-specific and varies with wave climate and beach morphology, but typically coincides with the region shoreward of persistent wave breaking. The dissipation of wave energy in the nearshore drives circulation, sediment transport, and morphological change. Comprehensive characterization of coastal phenomena therefore requires an understanding of the combined and interacting effects of ocean wave propagation with other hydrodynamic processes operating in the nearshore.
